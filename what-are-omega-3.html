What are omega-3 fatty acids?

A clear introduction to ALA, EPA and DHA—what they are, where they come from and why a biological role is not the same as a universal supplement benefit.

Published by TriOrigin LabsEditorially reviewedLast reviewed: 16 July 2026

Omega-3 is the name of a fatty-acid family. The three names most people encounter are ALA, EPA and DHA. They share a structural feature, but they are not interchangeable labels for the same molecule.

In brief

ALA is an essential omega-3 obtained from foods such as flaxseed, chia, walnuts and certain plant oils. EPA and DHA are long-chain omega-3s found mainly in seafood and marine or algal oils. The body can convert ALA toward EPA and DHA, but the conversion is limited.

What the name “omega-3” means

Fatty acids are chains of carbon atoms. “Omega-3” describes where the first carbon–carbon double bond appears when counted from the methyl end of that chain. It is a chemical classification—not a health verdict.

Many omega-3 fatty acids exist, but most human nutrition research focuses on alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA).

ALA, EPA and DHA at a glance

Fatty acidWhat distinguishes itCommon direct sources
ALAAn 18-carbon essential fatty acid; it must be obtained from the diet.Flax, chia, walnuts, canola and soybean oils.
EPAA 20-carbon long-chain omega-3 studied in several cardiovascular and inflammatory contexts.Fatty fish, seafood, fish oil and some algal oils.
DHAA 22-carbon long-chain omega-3 that is an important structural component of cell membranes and is especially concentrated in the brain and retina.Fatty fish, seafood, fish oil and algal oil.

Why ALA is called essential

The human body cannot create the omega-3 double-bond pattern from scratch. ALA must therefore come from the diet. U.S. dietary reference values specify adequate intakes for ALA; they do not establish a general RDA or Daily Value for EPA and DHA.

Can the body turn ALA into EPA and DHA?

Yes, but not efficiently. ALA can be converted to EPA and then toward DHA, mainly in the liver. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements describes the overall conversion as very limited and cites reported rates below 15%.

This does not make ALA unimportant. It means that a food rich in ALA should not be treated as if it supplies the same direct amount of EPA or DHA.

Where omega-3s come from

ALA is found mainly in plant foods and oils. EPA and DHA accumulate in fish and seafood, but the original producers in the marine food chain are microalgae. This is why algal oils can provide a direct non-fish source of DHA and, in some formulations, EPA.

What this does not prove

A nutrient can be biologically important without every supplement trial producing a health benefit. A study’s meaning depends on what was used, the amount, the population, the duration and the outcome measured. “Omega-3 is important” and “this product improves this clinical outcome” are different claims.

Medical boundary

This guide is general education. People who are pregnant, use anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication, have a bleeding disorder, have a diagnosed lipid disorder or are considering high-dose omega-3 should discuss their situation with a qualified clinician.
