EPA, DHA and ALA: what is the difference?

Compare the three principal omega-3 fatty acids by source, metabolism and evidence context—without turning the comparison into a simplistic winner.

Published by TriOrigin LabsEditorially reviewedLast reviewed: 16 July 2026

EPA, DHA and ALA are all omega-3 fatty acids, but comparing them requires more precision than asking which one is “best.” The right distinction depends on whether the question concerns dietary essentiality, direct sources, tissue structure, a biomarker or a studied clinical intervention.

Source is part of the question

Plant foods typically provide ALA. Seafood provides varying amounts of EPA and DHA. Algal oils can provide DHA and sometimes EPA. A label that says only “omega-3” leaves an important question unanswered: which fatty acids, and how much of each?

Conversion is limited, not zero

Human metabolism can elongate and desaturate ALA toward EPA and DHA, but this pathway is limited and varies. It is inaccurate to say ALA never converts; it is also inaccurate to count a gram of ALA as a gram of EPA or DHA.

Research findings follow the formulation

A trial using purified EPA cannot automatically establish the effect of a lower-dose mixed EPA/DHA supplement. A study of fish intake cannot isolate omega-3 from every other aspect of the food or dietary pattern. An outcome involving blood triglycerides is not the same as an outcome involving heart attacks or mortality.

Better question

Instead of “Which omega-3 is best?” ask: “For which population, source, dose, duration and outcome is each form being considered?”

What to look for on a label

Find the stated milligrams of EPA and DHA per serving. Do not assume the large “fish oil” number equals the amount of EPA plus DHA. Also check the serving size: a front label may imply a single capsule while the Supplement Facts panel defines a serving as two or more.
