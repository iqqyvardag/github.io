ALA, EPA and DHA compared

One family name can conceal three materially different fatty acids.

TriOrigin LabsLast assessed 17 July 20269 min read

On this page

ComparisonALAEPADHAConversionLabelsSources

ALA, EPA and DHA are often placed beneath one “omega-3” heading, but they differ in chain length, common sources, metabolism and the kinds of research questions in which they appear.

The core distinction

ALA is an essential 18-carbon fatty acid obtained mainly from plant foods. EPA and DHA are longer-chain fatty acids obtained directly from seafood and marine or algal oils, or produced in limited amounts from ALA.

ALA: essential does not mean equivalent

ALA is essential because the body cannot synthesize it. It contributes to total omega-3 intake and can act as the starting material for longer-chain omega-3 synthesis. However, this metabolic pathway is constrained, and conversion toward DHA is especially limited.

For that reason, adding together grams of ALA and grams of EPA or DHA without distinction can obscure what a person is actually consuming.

EPA: context matters

EPA is incorporated into lipid pools and can be converted into signalling molecules. In clinical research, EPA may appear alone or combined with DHA, at nutritional or pharmacological doses, in ordinary supplements or prescription medicines. Those contexts should not be merged automatically.

DHA: structure and life stage

DHA is an important structural fatty acid in cell membranes and is concentrated in tissues including the brain and retina. That biological role explains why DHA is studied in pregnancy, infancy, cognition and visual function. It does not, by itself, establish that supplementation improves every outcome in every population.

What changes conversion?

Conversion estimates vary according to study design and individual factors. Sex, hormonal status, background diet and competition for shared metabolic enzymes may influence the pathway. This is one reason percentage-conversion figures should be treated as approximate context rather than personal calculators.

Why the distinction matters on labels

A label may emphasize “1,000 mg fish oil” even when the EPA and DHA content is much lower. A plant oil may be rich in ALA but contain no direct EPA or DHA. Reading the named fatty acids—and the amount per serving—prevents the family name from hiding the dose.

Avoid the replacement shortcut

Do not assume that one gram of ALA produces one gram of EPA or DHA in the body. Do not assume that evidence from a prescription EPA product applies unchanged to a mixed retail fish-oil supplement.
