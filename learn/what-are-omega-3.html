What are omega-3 fatty acids?

ALA, EPA and DHA share a family name. Understanding their differences is the first step toward reading omega-3 evidence accurately.

TriOrigin LabsLast assessed 17 July 20268 min read

Omega-3 is the name of a fatty-acid family, not a single substance. The three names most people encounter are alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). They share a chemical feature, but they are not interchangeable.

In brief

ALA is an essential omega-3 found mainly in plant foods and oils. EPA and DHA are long-chain omega-3s found mainly in seafood and marine or algal oils. The body can convert ALA toward EPA and DHA, but the conversion is limited.

What “omega-3” means

Fatty acids are carbon chains. “Omega-3” describes the position of the first carbon–carbon double bond when counted from the methyl end of that chain. It is a chemical classification—not a judgement about whether a particular food or supplement improves health.

Many omega-3 fatty acids exist. Human nutrition discussions, however, usually centre on ALA, EPA and DHA.

The three principal omega-3s

ALA

Alpha-linolenic acid

An 18-carbon essential fatty acid. Common sources include flaxseed, chia, walnuts, soybean oil and canola oil.

EPA

Eicosapentaenoic acid

A 20-carbon long-chain omega-3 found in seafood and marine or algal oils. It is studied in several lipid and inflammatory contexts.

DHA

Docosahexaenoic acid

A 22-carbon long-chain omega-3 found in seafood and marine or algal oils. It is an important structural component of cell membranes.

Why ALA is essential

The human body cannot create the omega-3 double-bond pattern from scratch. ALA must therefore come from the diet. U.S. dietary reference values specify adequate intakes for ALA; they do not establish one general Recommended Dietary Allowance for EPA and DHA.

Can ALA become EPA and DHA?

Yes, through a sequence of elongation and desaturation reactions, mainly in the liver. But the overall conversion is limited. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements notes that reported conversion is very small, especially toward DHA.

This does not make ALA unimportant. It means that a food containing a given amount of ALA should not be described as though it supplies the same direct amount of EPA or DHA.

Food and supplement sources

ALA is found mainly in plant foods and oils. EPA and DHA accumulate in fish and seafood, while microalgae sit near the origin of these fatty acids in the marine food chain. Algal oils can therefore provide a direct non-fish source of DHA and, in some formulations, EPA.

What this information does not prove

A nutrient can be biologically important without every intervention trial producing the same clinical benefit. A result depends on the molecule, dose, formulation, population, duration, comparator and outcome. “Omega-3 has biological roles” and “this product improves this health outcome” are different statements.

Medical boundary

People who are pregnant, use medicines that affect clotting, have a bleeding disorder, have a diagnosed lipid disorder or are considering high-dose omega-3 should seek individualized advice from a qualified clinician.
