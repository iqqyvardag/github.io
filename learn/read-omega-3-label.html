How to read an omega-3 label

Separate the number that attracts attention from the amount that actually answers your question.

TriOrigin LabsLast assessed 17 July 20267 min read

On this page

Serving sizeEPA and DHAOil weightSource and formMarketing claimsComparison questionsSources

The largest number on an omega-3 bottle is often the least useful one. “Fish oil 1,000 mg” tells you the total oil weight—not necessarily how much EPA and DHA the serving provides.

Supplement Facts

Serving Size 2 Softgels

Fish oil concentrate2,000 mg
EPA600 mg
DHA400 mg

1Start with serving size. All amounts usually refer to the full serving, not one capsule.

2Find EPA and DHA. Here the serving provides 1,000 mg combined.

3Do not use total oil as the dose. The remaining oil includes other fatty acids and components.

Step 1: check serving size

A bottle may contain 120 softgels but define a serving as two, three or four softgels. Divide the listed EPA and DHA by the number of capsules only when you need the per-capsule amount.

Step 2: find EPA and DHA separately

Add the listed EPA and DHA when you need the combined amount. In the example above, 600 mg EPA plus 400 mg DHA equals 1,000 mg EPA+DHA per two-softgel serving.

If a label lists only “omega-3 fatty acids” without identifying EPA and DHA, it provides less information for comparison.

Step 3: separate oil weight from omega-3 content

Two products can both state “1,000 mg fish oil” while delivering very different amounts of EPA and DHA. Concentration is the proportion of the oil represented by the named omega-3 fatty acids.

EPA + DHA÷Total oil× 100= named EPA+DHA concentration

Step 4: identify source and form—without overreading them

Look for the source species or algae, chemical form if disclosed, and other ingredients. These details can matter, but they do not replace the actual dose or independent quality documentation.

Step 5: read front-label claims as claims

Words such as “ultra,” “maximum,” “triple strength,” “premium” and “pharmaceutical grade” may not have one standardized meaning on ordinary supplement labels. Return to the Supplement Facts panel and supporting documentation.

Five useful comparison questions

  1. How many capsules make one serving?
  2. How much EPA and DHA does that serving provide?
  3. What is the source and form?
  4. Is batch-specific testing available, and what was tested?
  5. What is the cost per meaningful EPA+DHA serving—not simply per capsule?

Do not turn a label into a personal prescription

A label can tell you what a serving contains. It cannot determine the right amount for a medical condition or establish whether the product is appropriate with your medicines.

Sources

  2. U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Information for consumers using dietary supplements.
