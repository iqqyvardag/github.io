Quality, oxidation and testing

A quality claim is most useful when the batch, method, result and limit can be inspected.

TriOrigin LabsLast assessed 17 July 202610 min read

On this page

OxidationPV, p-AV and TOTOXSpecificationsTest documentsStorageQuality languageSources

Omega-3 oils contain multiple double bonds, which makes them vulnerable to oxidation. Quality assessment therefore involves more than checking an expiry date or deciding whether a capsule smells “fishy.”

In brief

Peroxide value measures primary oxidation products; p-anisidine value measures certain secondary products in suitable oils; TOTOX combines them mathematically. These tests are useful but method-dependent and do not form a complete quality certificate by themselves.

What oxidation is

Oxidation is a sequence of chemical reactions in which unsaturated fatty acids form primary products such as hydroperoxides and then secondary breakdown products. Oxygen, heat, light, time, metals and repeated exposure after opening can influence the process.

The common measurements

PV

Peroxide value

Estimates primary oxidation products. A low value does not always prove an oil has never oxidized because peroxides can later decompose.

p-AV

p-Anisidine value

Estimates certain secondary aldehydes. The method may be unsuitable or require caution for strongly coloured or flavoured oils.

TOTOX

Total oxidation value

Usually calculated as 2 × PV + p-AV. It combines two indicators; it does not directly measure every oxidation compound.

What limits mean

Pharmacopoeias and voluntary industry monographs set specifications for particular oil categories and test methods. A result should be interpreted against the correct standard, product type and analytical method. It should also identify whether the test was performed on bulk oil, finished capsules or a flavoured product.

Passing an oxidation specification is important, but it does not by itself verify EPA/DHA content, contaminants, microbiology, capsule integrity or stability through the full shelf life.

What a useful test document should show

  • Product or batch identity that matches the bottle.
  • Laboratory name and test date.
  • Method used and units reported.
  • Actual result—not only “pass.”
  • Specification or limit used for comparison.
  • Tests for EPA/DHA identity and quantity, relevant contaminants and oxidation.

Freshness after purchase

Storage instructions matter. Keep the product tightly closed and follow the manufacturer’s temperature and light guidance. An unpleasant odour or taste may justify stopping use and contacting the manufacturer, but sensory impressions are not a substitute for analytical testing.

Quality language to treat cautiously

“Molecularly distilled,” “pharmaceutical grade,” “lab tested” and “third-party tested” can sound decisive while leaving key questions unanswered. Tested for what? By whom? On which batch? Against what specification? Was the result disclosed?

Transparency is stronger than a badge

The most useful quality claim is one that can be inspected: a batch identity, named laboratory, methods, numerical results, limits and a clear explanation of what the tests do not establish.
